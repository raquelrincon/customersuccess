Customer Success Guide for AI Companies

Introduction: You've Got Customers. Now What?

AI is booming. New companies, products, and tools are entering the market every day. Businesses across nearly every industry are exploring how AI can increase productivity, reduce costs, and improve efficiency. But with so many options out there, customers don't always know what's worth investing in, how to use it, or what sets one solution apart from another.

And you had an idea. You built a sweet AI product, and now you have customers. A whole portfolio of them.

But you're growing, and suddenly it's hard to give existing clients the CEO-level attention they've been used to.

That's okay! It's the direction you want your company to go. You just need to hand relationship management to someone else.

That's where Customer Success Managers come in. They build strong customer relationships, identify growth opportunities, prevent churn, and help customers get the most out of your product.

Building a Success team at an AI company comes with a few extra considerations.

Some customers are excited about AI. Others are skeptical, overwhelmed, or worried about what it means for their business and employees. And in a crazy twist, some may even become your competitors. Okay, not literally. But they may start exploring whether they can build their own alternatives with AI instead of paying for an existing product.

A great CSM helps customers cut through the AI noise, build trust, and understand what makes your product worth paying for.

There are plenty of titles for this role, including Partner Success Manager, Strategic Partner Manager, Enterprise or Technical CSM, Business Relationship Manager, and Client Partner. For simplicity, I'll use Customer Success Manager throughout this guide.

Whether you're hiring your first CSM or building an entire department, this guide will help you put the processes, expectations, and tools in place for your team to succeed.

This isn't a one-size-fits-all playbook. Every company has different customers, products, resources, and goals. The goal is to help you ask the right questions, make informed decisions, and build a CS function that works for your business.

Measuring ROI

Just to be clear, Customer Success is not Customer Support.

Your Success team will be a main point of contact for customers. They'll answer questions, help resolve technical and account-related issues, and make sure customers know where to turn for help. But that's secondary to their core function.

Customer Success drives revenue. Period.

Here are some of the ways your Success team contributes measurable value:

1. Retention and churn prevention

Keeping an existing customer is generally less expensive than acquiring a new one. CSMs can spot risks before they turn into cancellation requests, address adoption gaps, and help customers understand the value they're getting.

This is especially important for AI companies. Customers may be evaluating multiple tools, questioning whether they need a specialized solution, or wondering if they could build something similar in-house.

The goal is to protect recurring revenue by giving customers a reason to stay.

2. Contract renewals and annual price increases

Renewals are different from retention. If you've built annual price increases into your contracts, CSMs can help make sure they're implemented instead of falling through the cracks.

With enough notice, a clear explanation of the value, and thoughtful negotiation, CSMs can protect the customer relationship and your company's revenue.

3. Expansion revenue

As customers grow, their needs change. A CSM who understands their business can spot opportunities for additional products, workflows, users, or use cases.

For AI companies, those opportunities may exist beyond the department that originally purchased your product.

Expansion might mean moving to a higher plan, adopting another product, or finding new ways to get value from your existing solution.

4. New business and referrals

Happy customers can become a source of referrals, introductions, and qualified leads. CSMs can identify potential advocates and connect them with Sales to help generate new business.

But there's even more value they provide:

Not every contribution from Customer Success shows up as immediate revenue. Some of the team's most valuable work helps the company make better decisions, improve its product, and build a stronger reputation.

1. Testimonials, references, and case studies

Customers who achieve measurable results can help demonstrate your product's value to prospective buyers. CS can partner with Marketing to find customers willing to share their experiences and develop success stories.

2. Better product decisions

CSMs hear what customers love, what frustrates them, and what's getting in the way of value. A consistent feedback loop helps Product and Engineering prioritize improvements that benefit multiple customers, not just the loudest voice in the room.

3. Improved customer experience and operational efficiency

CSMs spot recurring friction across the customer journey, from onboarding to renewal. By working with Support, Product, and Operations to address it, CS can reduce avoidable support volume, improve adoption, and help customers get value faster.

Overview

There are a million different ways to build a Customer Success team.

So before you ask Claude to build you a CSM job description, take a step back and consider your immediate needs. What problems are you trying to solve? Where are your biggest gaps? And what do you actually need this person to own?

Start by asking yourself:

  • Are we experiencing significant churn?

  • Are customers struggling to adopt our product or realize its value?

  • Has our customer base grown beyond what the founders can personally manage?

  • Are we missing opportunities to expand existing accounts?

  • Are we struggling to manage renewals consistently?

  • Are internal teams unclear about who owns the customer relationship?

  • Are we spending too much time reacting to customer problems instead of preventing them?

  • Do we have a centralized place to track customer health, manage relationships, and give internal teams visibility into the customer journey?

Your answers will help define the type of CS role you need, its responsibilities, and what success should look like.

Measuring the Success of Success

You need to track the role's impact, so it makes sense to define its goals before you hire.

Choose KPIs that reflect the problems you're trying to solve. If the priority is reducing churn, retention should be a primary measure. If it's expansion, track expansion revenue.

Use the chart below to connect your business challenges to the metrics that can help measure progress.

If you’re struggling with… Measure success with…
Customer churn and lost revenue Gross Revenue Retention (GRR), customer churn rate, renewal rate, logo retention
Retention and expansion Net Revenue Retention (NRR), expansion ARR, upsell and cross-sell revenue, expansion pipeline, and product adoption
Slow onboarding or delayed customer value Time to Value (TTV), onboarding completion rate, time to first key outcome
Low product adoption Product adoption rate, feature utilization, active users, usage frequency
Poor customer experience Customer Satisfaction (CSAT), Net Promoter Score (NPS), Customer Effort Score (CES)
Inconsistent renewals Renewal forecast accuracy, renewal rate, on-time renewal rate

You don't need to measure every metric listed. Start with what makes sense for your business and establish a baseline with realistic targets.

I'd pay close attention to product adoption. AI solutions are still relatively new, and knowing how to use AI effectively is becoming a skill in itself. Many customers are still learning. Low usage doesn't automatically mean you have a bad product. Customers may need more guidance or education to understand how to use it well. A strong Success team can make a real difference here..

Defining Ownership

Before writing the job description, define what your CSM will own. This may mean shifting responsibilities from existing roles, most commonly Operations or Program Management.

This can be confusing for both your Operations team and your clients. I like to keep the distinction simple:

  • Customer Success owns the customer relationship. They're the liaison between the customer, their stakeholders, and the company. They make sure the customer knows who to contact, commitments are being met, contracts and renewals are managed, and the relationship continues to grow.

  • Operations owns the delivery relationship. They're the liaison between the service, product, or program being delivered and the customer's point of contact. They make sure delivery is running smoothly, teams are aligned, and the agreed-upon service is being executed.

Responsibilities will overlap, and that's okay. When introducing a CSM to a client, make it clear there's no wrong person to contact. Operations and CS work together to get each request into the right hands.

Both teams should communicate regularly and use a shared source of truth, whether that's a CRM, project management tool, or centralized account documentation.

At a minimum, both teams should be able to see:

Contract details, scope, and renewal dates.

Customer contacts, stakeholders, and escalation paths.

Customer goals, expectations, and commitments (particularly with AI).

Operational performance, KPIs, and SLAs.

Open issues, risks, and action items.

Upcoming changes, projects, and expansion opportunities.

You're not adding this role to create more handoffs. You're building a team that works together to deliver what was promised to the customer.

Here’s what that collaboration looks like in practice:

Scenario CS Action Operations Action
Customer wants to renegotiate their contract Leads the negotiation and meetings Supports with delivery costs/capacity
SLA performance is declining Communicates impact and manages relationship Investigates and resolves performance issues
Customer is unhappy with service quality Owns relationship recovery Creates corrective action plan
Customer is questioning the value of the AI product Understands concerns, reinforces business value, and coordinates the right conversations Provides product or technical context and helps address performance issues

Operations isn't the only department whose responsibilities may shift when you introduce a CSM. Account Managers and Account Executives may also be affected, especially if they currently own renewals, upselling, or commercial negotiations.

Before finalizing the role, involve the relevant departments. Make sure everyone understands what the CSM owns, what stays with existing roles, and where responsibilities overlap. You don't want two people assuming the other owns an important part of the customer relationship.

The Compensation

CSM compensation depends on the expectations of the role, its potential ROI, and how you'll measure success.

As a starting point, CSM compensation often includes a base salary and variable pay (bonus). An 80/20 split is one option: 80% base and 20% variable.

Some common ways to bonus CSMs include:

  • Gross Revenue Retention (GRR)

  • Net Revenue Retention (NRR)

  • Renewal rate

  • Expansion revenue

  • Customer adoption

  • Customer satisfaction (CSAT)

  • Overall company performance

This is a starting point, not a rule. The right structure depends on your company's needs and priorities. Tie compensation to responsibilities and outcomes the CSM can actually influence.

Customer Journey Survey

Use the questions below to guide conversations with internal teams. Mark each one Yes, No, or N/A. Note how the process works and who owns it. Anything missing or in need of improvement can become an action item.

Customer Journey Survey:

1. Customer information and ownership

  • Do we have a centralized location for customer information, such as a CRM or account management platform?

  • Is there a clearly defined owner responsible for maintaining customer records?

  • Do internal teams know who to contact when customer information needs to be added or updated?

  • Are customer goals, use cases, success criteria, and commitments documented?

  • Are customer contacts, stakeholders, decision-makers, and escalation paths documented?

  • Can relevant teams easily access the customer's contract, scope, and renewal date?

  • Is there a documented process for handing an account over when its owner changes?

2. Contract signature and handoff

  • Is there a consistent process for handing a signed contract from Sales to Customer Success?

  • Does the CSM receive the information they need before the customer kickoff?

  • Are the customer's goals, expectations, use cases, and any promises made during the sales process documented?

  • Is there a clear owner for scheduling and leading the kickoff?

  • Are internal teams notified when a new customer signs?

3. Onboarding and implementation

  • Is there a documented onboarding process for new customers?

  • Are onboarding milestones, responsibilities, and timelines defined?

  • Is there a clear owner for each implementation task?

  • Are customer requirements and technical dependencies documented before implementation begins?

  • Is there a process for collecting required data, credentials, and integrations securely?

  • Are security, privacy, and compliance requirements reviewed when applicable?

  • Are AI-specific requirements, such as data sources, intended use cases, human review, and acceptable output quality, documented?

  • Is there a process for testing the solution and confirming readiness before launch?

  • Do we define what successful onboarding and deployment look like?

  • Is there a process for communicating delays, blockers, or changes in scope to the customer?

4. Adoption and value realization

  • Do we have a defined process for helping customers adopt the product after deployment?

  • Are customer training and education responsibilities clearly assigned?

  • Do we track whether customers are using the product's key features or workflows?

  • Do we have a way to identify customers who are underusing the product or struggling to get value?

  • Do we regularly review progress against the customer's original goals and success criteria?

  • Is there a process for collecting feedback about AI output quality, accuracy, or reliability when relevant?

  • Do we help customers understand the product's capabilities and limitations?

  • Is there a clear process for identifying and documenting new use cases?

5. Support, issues, and escalations

  • Do customers know where to go when they need help?

  • Is there a documented process for routing customer issues to the right team?

  • Are escalation paths and response expectations clearly defined?

  • Is there a process for tracking unresolved issues and communicating updates to customers?

  • Are recurring issues reviewed with Product, Engineering, or Operations?

  • Is there a process for communicating product incidents or changes that may affect customers?

6. Renewals and account growth

  • Is there a documented renewal process with clear ownership?

  • Are CSMs automatically notified of upcoming renewals, ideally 60–90 days in advance?

  • Are customers notified of upcoming renewals according to a defined timeline?

  • Is there a process for reviewing customer health and renewal risk before the renewal date?

  • Is there a clear process for identifying and qualifying expansion opportunities?

  • When a customer wants to expand a program or add a use case, is it clear who owns the demo, pricing, pipeline entry, proposal, contract, and follow-through?

  • Is there a process for handing expansion opportunities to Sales or Account Management when appropriate?

  • Are renewal and expansion outcomes documented in the CRM?

But what good are these answers without action?

Use the results to build an improvement plan. Identify what needs to be defined or refined, assign an owner and due date to each action item, and agree on how you'll follow up.

Defining the Customer Lifecycle

Customer Success needs a repeatable way to guide customers from contract signature to ongoing value. The survey and follow-up actions should give you a clearer picture of your current customer lifecycle.

A typical lifecycle might look like this:

  • Sales handoff

  • Kickoff and discovery

  • Implementation and integration

  • Testing and launch

  • Adoption and value realization

  • Ongoing optimization

  • Renewal and expansion

Document your current process, define each stage, and identify its owner (including their title and email address). Keep this as a living internal reference for new hires and as teams change.

Tools, Software, and Solutions for Success

Your audit may have uncovered gaps in how customer information is stored, shared, and maintained. For example:

  • Calls aren’t recorded, or recordings aren’t stored somewhere the team can access.

  • Everyone uses a different note-taking tool, so finding call notes means checking four or five websites.

  • A customer expressed interest in expanding services, but there’s no shared record of the opportunity, its status, or who owns the next step.

  • Renewals are inconsistent because reminders aren't being sent.

The list goes on.

These are process problems the right tools can help solve. Before buying anything, review the software you already use. It may be able to meet your needs, or let you consolidate tools instead of adding another one.

The goal is a connected system where customer information is easy to find, conversations are documented, and teams know what needs to happen next.

CRM and Centralized Customer Information

Examples: Salesforce, HubSpot

Your CRM should be the central source of truth for customer and account information: key contacts, account history, contract details, renewal dates, opportunities, and important interactions.

At a minimum, anyone working with an account should be able to answer:

Who are the key stakeholders?

What did we agree to deliver?

What are the customer’s goals and current priorities?

What opportunities, risks, or open action items exist?

Who owns the next step?

You can also automate renewal tasks and communications. Choose a system that can use contract dates, assign tasks to the right owner, and log activity to the customer record.

Avoid scattering critical customer information across spreadsheets, personal notes, and inboxes. If it matters to the account, it belongs in a shared system.

Customer Success Platforms

Examples: Gainsight, ChurnZero, Planhat, Vitally

Customer Success platforms build on CRM data and offer tools specifically designed to manage post-sale relationships.

Depending on the platform, they can help teams:

  • Monitor customer health and engagement

  • Track onboarding, adoption, renewals, and expansion

  • Create automated workflows and playbooks

  • Identify accounts that may be at risk

  • Manage customer segments and CSM portfolios

  • Report on retention and other Customer Success metrics

These platforms become more useful as your customer base and CS team grow. A smaller team may be able to manage in its CRM until the need for a dedicated platform is clear.

Call Recording and Meeting Notes

Examples: Google Meet with Gemini, Gong, Zoom AI Companion, Fireflies.ai, Fathom

A consistent call recording and note-taking tool preserves customer context and makes conversations accessible to the people who need them.

Depending on the tool, it may provide:

  • Call recordings and searchable transcripts

  • Meeting summaries and action items

  • Links to recordings and notes on the customer’s CRM record

  • Conversation insights that help identify customer needs, risks, and opportunities

Whatever you choose, standardize on one approved tool and define where recordings and notes should live. Confirm recording consent, access permissions, and retention policies before rolling it out.

Knowledge Base and Internal Documentation

Examples: Confluence, Notion, Guru, Google Drive, SharePoint

A centralized knowledge base gives employees one place to find the information they need.

Use it to document:

  • Customer lifecycle stages and internal processes

  • Tool setup and how-to guides

  • Product documentation and troubleshooting steps

  • Templates for Mutual Success Plans, QBRs, and onboarding

  • Escalation paths, team responsibilities, and FAQs

Organize information so employees can find it quickly. Assign owners to key documents and review them regularly so outdated instructions don't become the default.

Project and Action-Item Management

Examples: Asana, ClickUp, Jira, Monday.com

Customer commitments often involve multiple teams. A project management tool can track action items, owners, deadlines, dependencies, and progress.

This is useful for onboarding, implementation, escalations, and cross-functional projects. It also makes it easier to follow through without relying on someone to remember what was discussed in a meeting.

For smaller teams, your CRM or existing collaboration tools may be enough. Don't add another task system unless it solves a real coordination problem.

Customer Communication and Collaboration

Examples: Slack, Microsoft Teams, email, shared customer portals

Choose clear, approved channels for customer communication and internal collaboration. If a customer uses Slack Connect or another shared channel, define who monitors it, expected response times, and where important decisions and commitments are documented.

Chat is convenient, but it shouldn't be the only place customer information lives. Capture key decisions, risks, and action items in your CRM or another shared source of truth.

Customer Feedback and Surveys

Examples: SurveyMonkey, Typeform, Qualtrics, built-in CRM or CS platform surveys

Feedback tools help you collect and analyze customer sentiment through CSAT, NPS, onboarding feedback, and other targeted surveys.

Use feedback to spot trends, uncover friction, and guide follow-up. Make sure someone reviews responses and closes the loop with customers. Collecting feedback without acting on it can erode trust.

Reporting and Analytics

Examples: Looker, Power BI, Tableau, CRM and CS platform dashboards

Reporting tools turn customer and operational data into useful insights. They can support dashboards for retention, expansion, adoption, customer health, support volume, and progress toward customer outcomes.

Start with a small set of metrics tied to business goals. Make sure the underlying data is reliable before investing heavily in dashboards.

Large Language Model (LLM)

Examples: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini

An LLM can help your CS team work more efficiently and get more out of the information it already has. Depending on your company's policies and tools, it can help:

Summarize calls, account history, and customer feedback

Draft follow-up emails, QBRs, and success plans

Turn meeting notes into action items

Analyze survey responses and identify recurring themes

Review a Mutual Success Plan for missing owners, dates, or unclear goals

Create or improve internal documentation and templates

With the right integrations, an LLM can also help employees find answers across approved company knowledge sources.

Treat AI output as a first draft, not a source of truth. Review it for accuracy, and never enter confidential customer or company information into an unapproved tool. Follow your organization's data security and privacy policies.

Mutual Success Plan

So, what's a Mutual Success Plan?

It may go by several different names, including Mutual Action Plan or Joint Success Plan. Whatever you call it, the purpose is the same: it's a living document that the Customer Success Manager and customer build and maintain together.

The plan uses your customer lifecycle as a starting point, but reflects each customer's goals, priorities, responsibilities, and timeline. The CSM and customer should revisit it, update it as things change, and use it to stay aligned.

A Mutual Success Plan isn't necessary for every customer. Use your judgment based on account size, complexity, and strategic importance. A simple action list may be enough for some customers; larger or more complex accounts may benefit from a shared plan.

The plan also gives your CSM a reason to get nosy. Beyond the goals of their main contact or department, it can uncover broader company priorities, dependencies, and opportunities to support other teams or initiatives.

At a minimum, the plan should capture:

  • Customer goals and desired outcomes

  • Key milestones and target dates

  • Action items, owners, and deadlines

  • Customer and CSM responsibilities

  • Risks, blockers, and dependencies

  • Progress toward success and value realization

Keep the plan somewhere both parties can access, such as your CS platform or a shared document. The customer and CSM should be the primary editors, with internal stakeholders given view access as needed. Store it in your central customer database so the wider team has context, and use it to prepare for business reviews and internal 1:1s.

AI can help review the plan for missing owners, unclear deadlines, or gaps between customer goals and action items. Just make sure the plan stays accurate and useful.

Introductions and Communication Cadence

A strong customer relationship starts with a thoughtful introduction and clear expectations. Here's a practical way to get a new CSM relationship started.

1. Arrange a warm introduction

Ask someone who already works regularly with the customer to introduce you to the key stakeholders. A warm introduction from a familiar face makes it easier to build trust.

2. Schedule the first Customer Success call

If you meet the stakeholder on a call, use that time to schedule a dedicated Customer Success follow-up. If the introduction happens over email, suggest a few meeting times.

Keep the invitation brief and include an agenda so the customer knows what to expect and why the meeting is worth their time.

3. Prepare before the call

Research the company, review the account history, and talk with internal teams who work with the customer. Use what you learn to prefill the Mutual Success Plan before the meeting.

This helps you ask informed questions instead of making the customer repeat information they've already shared.

4. Make the first Success call count

Use the call to:

  • Introduce yourself and explain your role in their account.

  • Share how they can make the best use of you and the Customer Success team.

  • Build rapport with a little small talk.

  • Review the Mutual Success Plan together.

  • Confirm or update their goals, priorities, stakeholders, responsibilities, and milestones.

Give the customer room to correct what you've gathered or fill in the gaps. The plan should reflect their priorities, not just your internal assumptions.

Get to know your main contact's manager or executive sponsor, too. AI can bring skepticism and competing priorities, so you may need to build that relationship over time. Keep your primary contact in the loop: address them directly and copy their manager when it makes sense. The goal is to make the work visible and collaborative, not to go around your contact. I always liked asking, “What can I do to make you look good?” Helping your contact show the value they're bringing to the business is part of the partnership. It's also why I recommend inviting their manager to business reviews.

For an AI company, ask which AI tools they've implemented, how those tools are working for them, and where they're considering using AI next.

5. Establish a regular meeting cadence

Recommend a recurring meeting cadence based on the account's needs, complexity, and current stage. Explain your reasoning and invite the customer to adjust it.

For a newly assigned account, I'd suggest weekly syncs for the first month. That gives the customer dedicated time with you while questions and action items are coming up. After that, move to monthly or twice-monthly meetings based on the account.

Keep the cadence flexible. Meet more often during implementation, major changes, or periods of risk. When the account is stable, fewer meetings may make more sense.

6. Confirm their preferred communication channel

Ask how the customer prefers to communicate between meetings. If they use Slack, get connected with them there. If they prefer email, confirm the best address to use.

Clarify what types of questions belong in each channel and what response times the customer can expect. This helps avoid mismatched expectations.

7. Follow up promptly

Send a follow-up email the same day as the call, or by the next morning. Include:

A brief recap of the discussion

Confirmed goals, decisions, and action items, including owners and due dates

The updated Mutual Success Plan

The next meeting date or agreed communication cadence

This gives both sides a shared record and a clear path forward.

8. Keep communication useful

No one likes a pointless calendar invite. Give recurring meetings a purpose: send a brief agenda, track action items, and share notes afterward. If the customer consistently has little to discuss, revisit the cadence instead of keeping meetings on the calendar out of habit.

The goal is to make communication predictable, useful, and easy for the customer.

Customer Health Scorecard

Before getting into the scorecard itself, let's cover what a Customer Health Score is and why you need one.

A Customer Health Score is an internal view of how an account is performing and whether the customer is getting value from your product or service. It brings together signals from across the relationship to show which accounts are healthy, which need attention, and where there may be room to grow.

A useful health score can help you spot risk and potential churn before the customer raises a concern. Sometimes the warning signs are there before the customer recognizes there's a problem.

And no, it shouldn't just measure whether the customer likes you. Sentiment matters, but it's only one part of the picture.

We hire CSMs because they're good with people. But a customer loving their CSM isn't the same as getting value from the product.

A customer might love their CSM, attend every meeting, and respond to every email, but still not get the results they expected. Another customer might be quiet, rarely attend meetings, and need very little hand-holding while getting tremendous value from your solution.

If you're only measuring the relationship, you're missing half the picture.

Building Your Scorecard

For an AI company, I'd build the scorecard around five key areas:

Product Performance and Outcomes

Is the product actually doing what it's supposed to do?

This is particularly important for AI companies because usage and automation numbers can look impressive on paper without necessarily translating into a good customer experience.

Look at the performance of the AI, how accurately it's completing its intended tasks, and whether it's delivering the business outcomes promised. Metrics like AI resolution rate, cost per resolution, and containment can tell a bigger story.

Adoption and Engagement

Are they actually using what they've purchased?

A customer can have a fantastic product with all the bells and whistles, but if they're only using a fraction of its capabilities, they may not be getting the full value of their investment.

For an AI company, I'd look at how much of the customer's eligible volume is being handled by the AI, how many workflows have been deployed, and whether adoption is expanding across the channels or departments identified in their success plan.

I'd also look at whether the customer is participating in training, optimization sessions, and other activities that help them get more out of the product. A lot of times, customers aren’t getting notified when new updates are happening (or they did, but forget and need a reminder.)

Relationship Health

Are you maintaining relationships with the right people? Do you have access to decision-makers? Are customers comfortable bringing concerns to you? And perhaps most importantly, do you know what's happening within their organization?

This is where you learn if they actually like and trust your company and the people involved.

Don't rely solely on how you feel a relationship is going. Look at the actual engagement and feedback you're receiving. While a customer might not have anything against you personally, a customer ignoring emails and not showing up to calls has a low engagement regardless.

And a customer showing up to calls, but coming with a list of complaints might have high engagement, but the happy sentiment isn’t there.

Operational Health

This is where you need to get your Operations, Technical Support, and Product teams involved.

Is the customer experiencing recurring technical issues? Are integrations reliable? Are escalations being resolved in a reasonable amount of time? Are there outstanding blockers preventing the customer from getting the results they expected?

Sometimes the customer relationship is perfectly fine, but the operational side of the account is struggling. Your CSM might be doing everything right, but if the product isn't working as expected, that will eventually affect the customer's perception of your company.

Commercial and Business Health

Finally, what's happening with the customer's business, and how does it affect your relationship?

A customer might be getting excellent results from your product, but if they've experienced budget cuts, a change in leadership, or a shift in company priorities, their renewal could still be at risk.

This is also where you can identify opportunities for growth. Are there additional departments that could benefit from your solution? Has the customer expressed interest in expanding services? Are there new initiatives that align with what you offer?

Next, decide how you'll score the metrics that matter to your business.

Start with a weighted scoring model. Not every category should carry the same weight. For an AI company, product performance and adoption may matter more than meeting attendance.

A 0–100 scale is one option, with three health categories:

Green (80–100): Healthy. The customer is achieving value, adoption is progressing, and there are no significant unresolved concerns.

Yellow (50–79): Needs attention. There are gaps in adoption, engagement, performance, or customer sentiment that need follow-up.

Red (0–49): At risk. There are significant concerns that could affect retention or the customer's ability to achieve their goals.

These thresholds are a starting point. Adjust them based on your customer data and experience.

Turn the Score Into Action

A health score is only useful if you act on it. Rally the right people, create an action-item list, and immediately assign an owner and due date to each item. Everyone should know what needs to happen next.

Document the risks, actions taken, and outcomes. Over time, this gives your team a record of what's working, where customers get stuck, and how to prevent similar issues.

Customer health is a team effort. The CSM may own the scorecard, but improving account health often takes Operations, Technical Support, Product, Finance, and other teams. Bring in the right people and keep them in the loop.

Don't worry about getting the scoring model perfect on the first try. You can keep refining it as your customer base grows and you learn more about what drives retention, adoption, and expansion.

Pay attention to the scores. If nearly every account is green, your criteria may be too lenient. If nearly every account is red, you may be weighting some categories too heavily. Use actual customer outcomes to keep calibrating the model.

Establishing Business Reviews

Business reviews are an important part of maintaining a healthy customer relationship. At a minimum, I recommend holding a quarterly business review (QBR), but don't wait three months to communicate with your customers. Operational updates and performance scores should be shared weekly, with a monthly business review to discuss progress, challenges, and anything that needs attention.

A good business review doesn't just read off scores or walk through a few slides. It tells the story of the partnership. It should highlight progress, uncover risks, identify opportunities for expansion, and give both sides a chance to talk about what's working and what could be better.

I recommend scheduling QBRs at least a month in advance. This gives your customer time to prioritize the meeting and gives you an opportunity to reinforce its importance. This is particularly helpful with customers who aren't always eager to attend business reviews.

Invite your main point of contact and their manager or executive sponsor. You should also be thoughtful about who joins from your own team. I like the rule of bringing one more person than the customer does. If they invite one person, bring two. If they invite two, bring three. It's a simple way to keep the meeting balanced while making sure the right people are in the room.

At a minimum, I'd recommend having the CSM and someone from Operations attend. If the customer brings a director or executive, consider bringing someone at a similar level from your own organization. Having a Director of Operations or another senior leader join can show that your company takes the partnership seriously and is invested in its success.

What to Include in a Business Review

Use your Mutual Success Plan as a starting point for the agenda. It gives you a natural way to revisit the customer's goals, review progress, and make sure everyone is still aligned.

Here are the areas I'd recommend covering:

Business Objectives and Progress

Revisit the customer's original business objectives and the action items you agreed to work on. What has been completed? Celebrate the wins. What is still outstanding? Talk about blockers and what needs to happen next.

Product Performance

Review how the product is performing and whether it's meeting expectations. This is where having someone from Operations involved is especially helpful. They can speak to performance, accuracy, recurring issues, and any operational trends.

Don't just present the numbers. Ask for feedback. If performance is strong, this may also be an opportunity to discuss what else the product could help them accomplish.

Business Impact and ROI

Product performance is only part of the story. You also want to understand the impact it's having on the customer's business. Has it saved time or money? Reduced workload? Improved efficiency? Identify measurable outcomes wherever possible.

Challenges, Feedback, and Open Issues

Discuss recurring issues, technical blockers, and customer feedback. More importantly, explain what you're doing about them.

You don't need to have an immediate fix for every problem, but you should always leave the customer knowing what happens next. If a chatbot is consistently giving incorrect answers, for example, acknowledge that it needs to be addressed, identify who you're working with internally, and give the customer a timeline for your next update.

Don't leave a customer with a problem and no plan.

Next Quarter's Action Plan

Now that you've reviewed the previous quarter, talk about what's coming next. Are the customer's business objectives still the same? Have their priorities changed? Are there upcoming deadlines, new initiatives, or opportunities to get more value from the product?

Revisit any milestones or performance goals you're working toward together. If a renewal is coming up next quarter, this is also a good time to discuss it and make sure there are no surprises.

Company Updates

I also like to include a slide for company-wide updates. These don't necessarily need to be specific to the customer. Share new product features, team or leadership changes, new integrations, or other company milestones. Even if a customer doesn't use a particular CRM integration, for example, it's still helpful for them to see that your product and company are continuing to grow.

Make It a Conversation

One of the biggest mistakes you can make during a business review is treating it like a presentation. Don't walk through every slide and end with, "Does anyone have any questions?" You might get a polite no, and that's the end of the conversation.

Instead, invite your customer to participate throughout the meeting. Share what you've noticed, explain what you're thinking, and ask for their perspective. Try phrases like, "We noticed this trend and would love to hear your thoughts," or "We've been looking at ways to improve this. What would make the biggest difference for your team?"

You want customers to feel comfortable sharing feedback, asking questions, and challenging ideas. The best business reviews are conversations, not presentations.

Don't wait until the week of the QBR to start putting everything together. I recommend having a complete draft ready at least one week before the meeting. Use the remaining time to review it with your internal team, practice the presentation, and identify any gaps or risks.

Rehearsing also gives you a chance to anticipate questions and make sure everyone is comfortable with the information they're presenting. If you uncover an issue during preparation, you have time to investigate it and come to the meeting with a plan. You don't want to discover a problem for the first time while you're presenting to the customer.

A little preparation goes a long way. Your customer should leave the meeting feeling informed, confident in the partnership, and clear on what happens next.

Overview

A good playbook turns a recurring problem into a repeatable process. If your team keeps running into the same customer challenges, you shouldn't have to figure out the solution from scratch every time.

This is one reason documenting action items throughout the customer journey is so important. Your Mutual Success Plan, customer health scorecard, meeting notes, and support tickets can all help you identify recurring issues, understand how they were handled, and see what actually worked.

AI can make this process even easier. By reviewing documented action items and their outcomes, you can use AI to identify patterns across accounts: which challenges come up most often, how frequently they happen, what solutions were attempted, and which ones were successful.

For example, if several customers are struggling with the same integration issue, you can look at how your team resolved it in the past and turn that knowledge into a documented process. Instead of having someone ask around or wait for another team member to explain what to do, they can open the playbook and get started.

The more consistently you document challenges, follow-ups, and outcomes, the more useful these insights become.

What Should You Create Playbooks For?

Start with the challenges your team encounters most often. Some common examples for AI companies include:

  • Low product adoption

  • Declining customer satisfaction

  • Renewal risks

  • Expansion opportunities

  • Stakeholder or point-of-contact turnover

  • Unresolved integration issues

  • AI performance below target

  • Recurring accuracy or incorrect-resolution issues

  • Confusing product configurations or workflows

You don't need a playbook for every possible scenario. Start with the problems that come up repeatedly or have a meaningful impact on the customer experience.

What Goes Into a Playbook?

Each playbook should give your team enough information to recognize the situation and know what to do next. I'd recommend including:

Warning Signs: What tells you this issue might be happening? Look at patterns you've noticed across accounts. For example, a customer repeatedly asking basic product questions might be a sign that they need additional training, even if they haven't directly asked for it.

Discovery Questions: What should your team ask to better understand the situation? If an account is at risk of not renewing, find out why. Is it a budget issue, low adoption, unmet expectations, or are they considering another provider? Understanding the reason helps you determine the right next step. If budget is the issue, for example, there may be an opportunity to scale back the solution rather than lose the customer entirely.

Recommended Actions: Based on what's worked in the past, what steps should your team take? Include the teams or individuals who may need to be involved.

Follow-Up and Outcomes: How should the issue be tracked, and what does a successful resolution look like? Document what worked, what didn't, and any lessons that could help the next person handling a similar situation.

Creating a Voice of the Customer Feedback Loop

Playbooks help your team respond to recurring challenges, but you also need a way to share those patterns with the rest of the business.

Customer Success is in a unique position to hear directly from customers about what's working, what's frustrating them, and what they wish the product could do. That feedback shouldn't stay buried in meeting notes or individual customer accounts.

For AI companies, this is particularly important. If customers are repeatedly reporting inaccurate AI responses, struggling with integrations, or finding configurations confusing, those patterns should be shared with Product, Engineering, Technical Support, and leadership. A single complaint may be an isolated issue. The same complaint across multiple accounts could point to a much bigger problem.

I recommend creating a regular meeting or reporting process to review customer feedback and recurring themes. Depending on your team, this could be a weekly or monthly meeting with the relevant departments. Your CRM, ticketing system, and centralized customer database can also help capture and organize feedback so it doesn't get lost.

AI can help here, too. You can use it to review customer notes, support tickets, meeting summaries, and feedback to identify common themes across accounts. The goal is to give your internal teams a clearer picture of what customers are experiencing and where improvements are needed.

Most importantly, close the loop. When feedback leads to a product improvement, process change, or resolution, document it and let the customer know. Customers want to know they're being heard, and following up shows that their feedback actually makes a difference.

Where Should You Store Your Playbooks?

Once you've created a playbook, make sure it's somewhere your team can actually find it. Your knowledge base is a great place to start. If you don't have one set up yet, revisit the Tools, Software, and Solutions for Success section for some suggestions.

You can also use another centralized location, as long as the team knows where to find it and has access to the latest version. Keep playbooks easy to update as your product, processes, and customer needs evolve.

A playbook that nobody can find isn't doing anyone much good.

Final Thoughts

You've created the foundation for your Customer Success function, but this is just the beginning. As your company grows, your CS team and processes will grow with it.

You'll start to understand which customers need more hands-on support, how much time different accounts require, and where your CSMs are spending their time. You may eventually segment customers into strategic, enterprise, or other tiers and assign CSMs based on their needs. You'll also get a better understanding of your team's capacity and the return on that investment. Don't assume that 100 accounts always require the same number of CSMs. Two companies can have the same number of customers and very different support needs.

As your team becomes more established, you'll also start noticing opportunities to work smarter. Maybe you can automate meeting reminders, make meeting preparation easier, trigger faster customer health alerts, or give CSMs better insights into account activity. Pay attention to the tasks your team repeats and look for ways to make those processes more efficient.

Most importantly, keep reviewing your processes. Your customer base will change, your product will evolve, and your customers' expectations will change along with it. What works today may need to be adjusted six months from now. Keep measuring results, documenting what you learn, and making improvements as you go.

And remember why you're building this function in the first place. Customer Success shouldn't exist simply because you have enough customers to justify another department. It should exist because your business recognizes the value of helping customers achieve meaningful outcomes.

When customers see value, they're more likely to stay, renew, expand, and recommend your product. They become references, advocates, and champions who will happily tell others how much they love working with you. That's a real contribution to the business, and it's one worth investing in.

Start with the business problem you're trying to solve. Define what success looks like, build the processes to support it, and measure the results. You don't need a perfect Customer Success department on day one. You just need a purpose, a plan, and a willingness to keep improving.

Now go build something great!
