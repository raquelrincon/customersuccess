Customer Success Guide for AI Companies

Introduction: You've Got Customers. Now What?

You had an idea. You built a product. And now you have customers. A PORTFOLIO of customers.

But you're growing. Suddenly, it's hard to give your existing clients the CEO-level attention they've been getting.

That's okay! That's the direction you want your company to go. It doesn't mean you have to stop giving them top-notch attention. It means you need a repeatable way to deliver it.

There are people who specialize in building strong customer relationships, identifying opportunities for growth, preventing churn, and helping customers get the most out of your product. They're your future Customer Success Managers.

If you’re building an AI company, there are other things to consider when building your Success team.

AI is booming. New companies, products, and tools are entering the market every day. Businesses across nearly every industry are trying to figure out how they can use AI to increase productivity, reduce costs, and improve efficiency. But with so many options available, customers don't always know what's worth investing in, how to use it, or what separates one solution from another.

Some customers are excited about AI. Others are skeptical, overwhelmed, or worried about what it means for their business and employees. And in a crazy twist, some may even become your competitors. Okay, not literally. But they are starting to explore building their own alternatives using AI rather than paying for an existing product.

This is why building a success team early on is important. A great CSM will help customers cut through the AI noise, build trust, and show customers what makes your product worth paying for.

There are several different titles used in place of Customer Success Manager, including: Partner Success Manager, Strategic Partner Manager, Enterprise/Strategic/Technical Customer Success Manager, Business Relationship Manager, Client Partner, etc. But for the sake of keeping it simple, we will refer to this role as Customer Success Manager for the duration of the guide.

Whether you're hiring your first CSM or building an entire department, this guide will help you establish the processes, expectations, and tools your team needs to succeed.

This isn't a one-size-fits-all playbook. Every company has different customers, products, resources, and goals. The objective is to help you ask the right questions, make informed decisions, and build a CS function that makes sense for your business.

Overview

Maybe you're at the stage where you know you need someone to protect recurring revenue, but you still have to prove it to the people holding the budget.

Finance lives by the motto: "If it doesn't make dollars, it doesn't make cents."

Fair enough.

So let's get you ready for your elevator pitch and the inevitable Q&A session with finance.

You have just a couple of minutes to win someone over. Lead with the benefits, not the cost. Explain how a Customer Success team will protect and grow revenue, improve retention, and create expansion opportunities. Then show how you'll measure the team's ROI.

If there's time, walk through your execution plan, including your timeline, the resources you'll need, and the tools you'll use to get the team up and running.

And know your plan front to back. Because once you make the pitch, the questions will come.

Types of questions to be prepared for:

  • What is the cost of doing nothing?

  • How much revenue is at risk today?

  • What does success look like in the first 90 days?

  • How much will it cost to build and operate the team?

The next couple of sections will help you prepare your answers.

Measuring ROI

In case it isn't clear, Customer Success is not Customer Support.

Of course, your Success team will become a main point of contact for your customers. They'll answer questions, help resolve technical and account-related issues, and make sure customers know where to turn when they need help. But that's secondary to their actual function.

Customer Success generates revenue. Period.

When it comes to measurable, monetary value, your Success team contributes in several ways:

1. Retention and churn prevention

Keeping an existing customer is generally less expensive than acquiring a new one. CSMs can identify risks before they become cancellation requests, address gaps in product adoption, and help customers understand the value they're receiving.

This is particularly important for AI companies. Customers may be evaluating multiple tools, questioning whether they need a specialized solution, or wondering if they could build something similar internally.

The goal is to protect recurring revenue by giving customers a reason to stay.

2. Contract renewals and annual price increases

This is different from retention. Hopefully, you've built annual price increases into your contracts. CSMs help ensure those increases are actually implemented instead of falling through the cracks.

By providing ample notice, communicating the value behind the increase, and managing the negotiation, CSMs can protect both the customer relationship and your company's revenue.

3. Expansion revenue

As customers grow, their needs change. A CSM who understands their business can identify opportunities for additional products, workflows, users, or use cases.

For AI companies, expansion opportunities may also exist outside the department that originally purchased your product.

These opportunities might come from a customer outgrowing their current plan, adopting a new product, or discovering a way to get even more value from your existing solution.

4. New business and referrals

Happy customers can become a source of referrals, introductions, and qualified leads. CSMs can identify customers who are willing to advocate for your company and connect them with Sales to help generate new business.

But there's even more value they provide:

Not every contribution from Customer Success will show up as immediate revenue. Some of the team's most valuable work helps your company make better decisions, improve its product, and build a stronger reputation.

1. Testimonials, references, and case studies

Customers who achieve measurable results can help demonstrate your product's value to prospective buyers. CS can partner with Marketing to identify customers who are willing to share their experiences and develop compelling success stories.

2. Better product decisions

CSMs hear what customers love, what frustrates them, and what prevents them from getting value. A consistent feedback loop helps Product and Engineering prioritize improvements that benefit multiple customers, not just the loudest voice in the room.

3. Improved customer experience and operational efficiency

CSMs identify recurring friction points across the customer journey, from onboarding to renewal. By working with Support, Product, and Operations to address those issues, CS can reduce avoidable support volume, improve adoption, and help customers get value faster.

Overview

There are a million different ways to build a Customer Success team. CS roles aren't a monolith, and what works for one company might not work for yours.

So before you ask Claude to build you a CSM job description, take a step back and consider your immediate needs. What problems are you trying to solve? Where are your biggest gaps? And what do you actually need this person to own?

Start by asking yourself:

  • Are we experiencing significant churn?

  • Are customers struggling to adopt our product or realize its value?

  • Has our customer base grown beyond what the founders can personally manage?

  • Are we missing opportunities to expand existing accounts?

  • Are we struggling to manage renewals consistently?

  • Are internal teams unclear about who owns the customer relationship?

  • Are we spending too much time reacting to customer problems instead of preventing them?

  • Do we have a centralized place to track customer health, manage relationships, and give internal teams visibility into the customer journey?

Your answers will help you determine what kind of CS role you need, what responsibilities it should own, and what success should look like.

Measuring the Success of Success

Your KPIs should reflect the problems you're trying to solve. If you're building a CS team to reduce churn, retention should be a primary measure of success. If you're hiring to drive expansion, you need to track expansion revenue.

Use the chart below to connect your business challenges to the metrics that will help you measure progress.

If you’re struggling with… Measure success with…
Customer churn and lost revenue Gross Revenue Retention (GRR), customer churn rate, renewal rate, logo retention
Retention and expansion Net Revenue Retention (NRR), expansion ARR, upsell and cross-sell revenue, expansion pipeline, and product adoption
Slow onboarding or delayed customer value Time to Value (TTV), onboarding completion rate, time to first key outcome
Low product adoption Product adoption rate, feature utilization, active users, usage frequency
Poor customer experience Customer Satisfaction (CSAT), Net Promoter Score (NPS), Customer Effort Score (CES)
Inconsistent renewals Renewal forecast accuracy, renewal rate, on-time renewal rate

You do not need to measure every metric listed. Start with what makes sense for your business, establish a baseline with realistic targets.

Personally, I’d pay close attention to product adoption. AI solutions are still relatively new, and knowing how to use AI effectively is becoming a skill in itself. Many of your customers are still learning. A customer who isn't using your product isn't necessarily a sign that you have a bad product. They may simply need more guidance, education, or support to understand how to use it effectively. Building a strong Success team can be the key to driving adoption.

Defining Ownership

Before building a job description, define which responsibilities your Customer Success Manager will own. This may mean shifting responsibilities from existing roles to the CSM. The role most commonly impacted is Operations or Program Management.

This can be confusing for both your Operations team and your clients. I always keep the distinction simple:

  • Customer Success owns the customer relationship. They're the liaison between the customer, their stakeholders, and the company. They make sure the customer knows who to contact, commitments are being met, contracts and renewals are managed, and the relationship continues to grow.

  • Operations owns the delivery relationship. They're the liaison between the service, product, or program being delivered and the customer's point of contact. They make sure delivery is running smoothly, teams are aligned, and the agreed-upon service is being executed.

Responsibilities can overlap, and that's okay. When introducing a CSM to a client, make it clear that there's no wrong person to reach out to. Your Operations team and CSM work closely together, and they'll make sure every request gets into the right hands.

Because that's how it should work! Both teams should communicate regularly and have a shared source of truth they can refer to. Whether that's a CRM, project management tool, or centralized account documentation, everyone should be working from the same information.

At a minimum, both teams should have visibility into:

  • Contract details, scope, and renewal dates.

  • Customer contacts, stakeholders, and escalation paths.

  • Customer goals, expectations, and commitments (particularly with AI).

  • Operational performance, KPIs, and SLAs.

  • Open issues, risks, and action items.

  • Upcoming changes, projects, and expansion opportunities.

The goal isn't to create more handoffs. It's to create a team that works together to deliver on what was promised to the customer.

Working together is imperative. Take these real-world scenarios:

Scenario CS Action Operations Action
Customer wants to renegotiate their contract Leads the negotiation and meetings Supports with delivery costs/capacity
SLA performance is declining Communicates impact and manages relationship Investigates and resolves performance issues
Customer is unhappy with service quality Owns relationship recovery Creates corrective action plan
Customer is questioning the value of the AI product Understands concerns, reinforces business value, and coordinates the right conversations Provides product or technical context and helps address performance issues

One thing I’d like to note: Operations isn't the only department whose responsibilities may shift when you introduce a CSM. Account Managers and Account Executives may also be affected, particularly in organizations where they own renewals, upselling, or commercial negotiations.

Before you finalize the role, get all the relevant departments involved. Make sure everyone understands what the CSM will own, what stays with existing roles, and where responsibilities overlap. Otherwise, you risk creating confusion internally and an inconsistent experience for your customers. The last thing you want is two people assuming the other one owns an important part of the customer relationship.

The Compensation

What you choose to pay a CSM is up to you. You know the expectations of the role, the potential ROI of adding it, and how you'll measure their success.

As a general starting point, CSM compensation typically includes a base salary plus variable compensation (bonus). An 80/20 split is a good place to start, meaning 80% base salary and 20% variable.

Some common ways to bonus CSMs include:

  • Gross Revenue Retention (GRR)

  • Net Revenue Retention (NRR)

  • Renewal rate

  • Expansion revenue

  • Customer adoption

  • Customer satisfaction (CSAT)

  • Overall company performance

This is simply a suggestion. The right structure will depend on your company's needs and the priorities you identified earlier. Just make sure you're tying compensation to responsibilities and outcomes the CSM can actually influence.

Overview

Before you start building new processes, take a look at the ones you already have.

One of the first things your Customer Success team should do is map the customer journey, from contract signature through onboarding, deployment, adoption, renewal, and expansion. How does a customer move through each stage today? Where do handoffs get stuck? Are important details being documented in a centralized place, or are they scattered across emails, Slack messages, and individual spreadsheets? (I became an expert at sifting through our company’s Google Drive…but no one should have to do that.)

I recommend making this one of your CSM's first projects after they've had a chance to get familiar with your product. It's a great way for them to meet people across the organization, understand how teams work together, and identify opportunities to improve the customer experience.

This is another way CSMs bring value to an organization. They help teams understand what's working, uncover gaps, and make sure every stage of the customer journey has clear ownership.

Customer Journey Survey

Use the questions below as a starting point for conversations with your internal teams. For each question, mark Yes, No, or N/A. If the answer is yes, document how the process works and who owns it. If the answer is no, or the process needs improvement, capture it as a potential action item.

Not every question will apply to every company. Adapt the survey to your product, customer base, and existing workflows.

Bonus: An Excel/Google Sheets version of this survey is attached to this playbook and available for download.

Customer Journey Survey:

1. Customer information and ownership

  • Do we have a centralized location for customer information, such as a CRM or account management platform?

  • Is there a clearly defined owner responsible for maintaining customer records?

  • Do internal teams know who to contact when customer information needs to be added or updated?

  • Are customer goals, use cases, success criteria, and commitments documented?

  • Are customer contacts, stakeholders, decision-makers, and escalation paths documented?

  • Can relevant teams easily access the customer's contract, scope, and renewal date?

  • Is there a documented process for handing an account over when its owner changes?

2. Contract signature and handoff

  • Is there a consistent process for handing a signed contract from Sales to Customer Success?

  • Does the CSM receive the information they need before the customer kickoff?

  • Are the customer's goals, expectations, use cases, and any promises made during the sales process documented?

  • Is there a clear owner for scheduling and leading the kickoff?

  • Are internal teams notified when a new customer signs?

3. Onboarding and implementation

  • Is there a documented onboarding process for new customers?

  • Are onboarding milestones, responsibilities, and timelines defined?

  • Is there a clear owner for each implementation task?

  • Are customer requirements and technical dependencies documented before implementation begins?

  • Is there a process for collecting required data, credentials, and integrations securely?

  • Are security, privacy, and compliance requirements reviewed when applicable?

  • Are AI-specific requirements, such as data sources, intended use cases, human review, and acceptable output quality, documented?

  • Is there a process for testing the solution and confirming readiness before launch?

  • Do we define what successful onboarding and deployment look like?

  • Is there a process for communicating delays, blockers, or changes in scope to the customer?

4. Adoption and value realization

  • Do we have a defined process for helping customers adopt the product after deployment?

  • Are customer training and education responsibilities clearly assigned?

  • Do we track whether customers are using the product's key features or workflows?

  • Do we have a way to identify customers who are underusing the product or struggling to get value?

  • Do we regularly review progress against the customer's original goals and success criteria?

  • Is there a process for collecting feedback about AI output quality, accuracy, or reliability when relevant?

  • Do we help customers understand the product's capabilities and limitations?

  • Is there a clear process for identifying and documenting new use cases?

5. Support, issues, and escalations

  • Do customers know where to go when they need help?

  • Is there a documented process for routing customer issues to the right team?

  • Are escalation paths and response expectations clearly defined?

  • Is there a process for tracking unresolved issues and communicating updates to customers?

  • Are recurring issues reviewed with Product, Engineering, or Operations?

  • Is there a process for communicating product incidents or changes that may affect customers?

6. Renewals and account growth

  • Is there a documented renewal process with clear ownership?

  • Are CSMs automatically notified of upcoming renewals, ideally 60–90 days in advance?

  • Are customers notified of upcoming renewals according to a defined timeline?

  • Is there a process for reviewing customer health and renewal risk before the renewal date?

  • Is there a clear process for identifying and qualifying expansion opportunities?

  • When a customer wants to expand a program or add a use case, is it clear who owns the demo, pricing, pipeline entry, proposal, contract, and follow-through?

  • Is there a process for handing expansion opportunities to Sales or Account Management when appropriate?

  • Are renewal and expansion outcomes documented in the CRM?

But what good are these answers without action?

Once you've completed the survey, use the results to build an improvement plan. Identify the processes that need to be defined or refined, assign an owner and due date to each action item, and establish a plan to follow up.

Defining the Customer Lifecycle

Customer Success should have a repeatable approach to guiding customers from contract signature to ongoing value realization. Completing the survey and addressing the action items should give you a clearer picture of your customer lifecycle.

A typical lifecycle might look like this:

  1. Sales handoff

  2. Kickoff and discovery

  3. Implementation and integration

  4. Testing and launch

  5. Adoption and value realization

  6. Ongoing optimization

  7. Renewal and expansion

Start by documenting your current process. Define each stage and identify who owns it, including their job title and email address. This should be a living document for internal use. It can serve as a reference for new hires and help teams navigate organizational changes.
